Research and publications

Overview

My research focuses on the development of second language (L2) speech. I am especially interested in how new phonetic-phonological patterns emerge and change over time, and in the role that variability and individual differences play in this process.

To capture fine-grained changes in speech, I use multi-wave longitudinal designs, often combined with periods of L2 instruction. In my MA thesis, I am exploring Bayesian Generalized Additive Mixed Models (GAMMs) as a way to model changes in vowel production within multilingual systems.

Beyond production, I also investigate how listeners rate non-native speech for accentedness and comprehensibility, and how learners’ attitudes towards varieties of English shape their pronunciation learning.

Brazilian learners’ development of English-L2 pre-fortis clipping patterns: a process-oriented study through Complex Dynamic Systems Theory

Arthur Trein, Ubiratã Alves, Felipe Kupske and Luana Gauer · Under review

Pre-print   Data  

Complex Dynamic Systems Theory (CDST) has proven an important epistemological framework for analyzing non-native language phonetic-phonological development. This conceptual framework entails specific methodological considerations, especially when it comes to experimental designs in which developmental trajectories are investigated over time, in process oriented studies. Drawing on a complex-dynamic view of language in association with the Speech Learning Model, the present study aims to investigate the development of the pre-fortis clipping pattern of vowels preceding plosive consonants in English by Brazilian learners. Three participants were tracked longitudinally in 12 data collection points over 13 weeks. Between the first and second data collection point, participants received different types of linguistic input: perceptual training, perceptual training combined with explicit pronunciation instruction, and no pedagogical intervention. Participants’ productions of the target form were acoustically analyzed and tested through Peak Analyses with Monte Carlo Simulations and Change Point Analyses. Results indicate different individual patterns of variability and change, potentially linked to the distinct pedagogical treatments present in the study. The findings underscore the relevance of process-oriented studies for analyzing non-native language phonetic-phonological development, as well as the conceptual compatibility of CDST with the Speech Learning Model. They also highlight the affordances associated with the complementary employment of the two data analysis methods featured in the study.

Variability in the development of phonetic-phonological patterns by L2 learners: analyzing length gains in vowels preceding final voiced plosives in L2 English

Ubiratã Alves, Arthur Trein and Luana Gauer · Letras de Hoje

Access  

In Complex Dynamic Systems Theory (CDST), intra-individual variability plays a functional role in the development of new patterns and skills in a Second Language (L2), serving as an indicator of developmental progress. In a recent study, Verspoor and de Bot (2022) argue that higher levels of variability are associated with greater developmental gains during periods of intensive earning. The authors propose the use of two measures – the Coefficient of Variation (CoV) and the Standard Deviation of Differences (SDd) – to quantify and compare variability across datasets involving multiple learners. The present exploratory study employs these measures to assess developmental gains related to a phonetic-phonological phenomenon in English as an L2. Drawing on longitudinal data from 11 Brazilian learners, we aim to investigate whether variability (operationalized as CoV and SDd) correlate with vowel length gains preceding the voiced plosives /b d g/ in syllable-final position in L2 English. In 12 recording sessions, participants produced monosyllabic words ending in voiced stop consonants. Between the first and second data collection points, learners – divided into three groups – received different pedagogical tre-atments: perceptual training only, perceptual training combined with explicit pronunciation instruction, or no training/instruction. Following the acoustic analysis, we calculated (a) vowel length gains for each stop consonant, (b) the CoV, and (c) the SDd of vowel length for each participant at each datapoint. Correlation analyses indicate that variability is positively associated with the increase in vowel length over the course of the study. These findings highlight the relevance of intra-individual variability in the development of new pronunciation patterns in the L2, especially in periods of accelerated learning.

Development of speech fluency in an additional language: Analysis of breakdown rates in the speech of an Argentine learner of Brazilian Portuguese

Ubiratã Alves, Pollianna Milan, Arthur Trein, Cíntia Raquel de Moraes, Felipe Vieira and Laryssa Alves · In book Cuestiones del lenguaje en contextos académicos y profesionales: enseñanza y traducción

Access  

This study investigates the longitudinal development of breakdown fluency in the speech of an advanced Argentine learner of Brazilian Portuguese (L3). The analysis is grounded in Complex Dynamic Systems Theory (CDST), which views language as a non-linear and adaptive system. We analyzed 24 audio recordings of spontaneous speech collected over one year. The study adopted an A-B-A methodological design, with a period of explicit pronunciation instruction between recording sessions 10 and 15. Breakdown fluency was operationalized through measures of silent and filled pauses in both mid-clause and end-of-clause contexts. The results of the Peak Analysis with Monte Carlo Simulations revealed no abrupt changes, which may be attributed to the learner’s high proficiency. However, a Change Point Analysis showed a significant decrease in the duration and quantity of silent end-of-clause pauses. This suggests that even advanced learners continue to develop fluency, and that these changes can occur gradually. The findings also align with previous research indicating that pronunciation instruction may have a positive impact on fluency development.

Brazilian Students’ Attitudes Towards Model Varieties of English: Implications for Learner Identity and L2 Pronunciation Development

Ubiratã Alves, Ronaldo Lima Jr. and Arthur Trein · In book (Mis)Understandings in Multicultural Communication

Access  

Attitudes towards language varieties and their communities often impact non-native speech development. Their influence is especially striking when it comes to the English language, whose global spread and teaching practices have been decisively shaped by native model prescriptions. Considering this scenario, previous studies have investigated learners’ attitudes towards the two most traditional English pronunciation models, General American (GenAm) and Received Pronunciation (RP), revealing meaningful attitudinal tendencies towards these native varieties. To extend this body of work, we asked 37 advanced Brazilian learners of English to complete a language attitude questionnaire and, using a Verbal Guise Technique, we measured their cognitive, affective, and conative evaluations of two North American English and two British English speakers. Scores from 6-point Likert scales for 11 attitudinal traits revealed a tendency for learners to emulate, and greater identify with, GenAm, while ascribing qualities such as intelligence and responsibility to RP. These results offer insights into how learners infer social meaning from linguistic forms and negotiate their identities through L2 speech. They also add to the debate on how language ideologies shape L2 pronunciation learning. The chapter concludes with implications for future research and practice.

Accentedness perception ratings in non-native speaker-listener binomials: Analyzing the influence of proficiency

Arthur Trein, Ubiratã Alves and Pedro Luchini · In book Otras Cuestiones del Lenguaje: Traducción y enseñanza de lenguas extranjeras

Access  

Whether or not to focus L2 pronunciation instruction in English on “eradicating” learners’ foreign accent has been the core question of different pedagogical ideologies. However, teachers and learners are still highly influenced by native models of pronunciation. Given this scenario and the importance of understanding more about the nature of the accentedness construct, this paper aims to investigate the influence of both speakers’ and listeners’ characteristics, specifically non-native proficiency, on accentedness perception. To this aim, five Argentinian speakers of English, ranked in terms of pronunciation proficiency from low to high, were assessed by 40 Brazilian learners of English divided into three general proficiency groups. Resulting scores of 9-point Likert scales representing accentedness levels were explored through a mixed-effects model, with ‘speaker’ and ‘listener group’ as predictor variables. The statistical test revealed a significant effect of the ‘speaker’ variable for the most proficient speaker, followed by a marginally significant interaction of the ‘speaker’ and ‘listener group’ variables for the same speaker and the most proficient listener group. Results indicate that accentedness judgements depend on both speakers’ and listeners’ characteristics, and that non native language learners may rely on the similarity with native models for the assessment of their fellow learners’ speech. Some pedagogical implications of this study for the teaching of L2 pronunciation are thoroughly discussed.
